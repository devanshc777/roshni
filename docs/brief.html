The original idea

Problem statement 17

The problem statement says repair queues follow first-come complaint logs without factoring in nighttime footfall, so dark arterial streets carrying late-night commuters go unprioritised. It asks for a pedestrian-centric reporting and routing system, a public dark-zone risk map that weights repairs by pedestrian exposure, and safer alternate routes for night walkers. Then it asks the hard one: how would you validate a footfall estimate for a street with no official usage data at all?

Our answer starts from a measured fact rather than an assumption. BBMP closes 96.4% of electrical complaints. They are good at fixing and bad at ordering — because the queue is sorted by who complained first, and complaining tracks affluence and app-literacy, not how many people walk that street at 11pm.

So Roshni is not a lighting system and not a reporting app. It is a re-ordering layer that sits on the queue a city already has. Nobody has to work harder or buy anything. They need the list in a different order.

How it connects

One estimate, two decisions

Every screen reads from a single darkness estimate per 200 m of street, each with a confidence band. That is the whole architecture, and it is why the screens are genuinely connected rather than four demos in a row: a report changes the estimate, and both outputs move because they are reading the same number.

01 — INPUT

What arrives

A road network is the only hard requirement. Ward boundaries, lamp counts, the complaint log and the transit feed are each optional. Plus one tap from a citizen: “this stretch is dark.”

02 — MODEL

One shared estimate

A darkness probability and a confidence band per segment, from a beta-binomial posterior. 94% of segments run on priors alone — that is the normal case, not a failure case.

03 — OUTPUT A

The walker

Exposure divides the route penalty. Busy streets are preferred; dark and unknown ones are avoided. Two routes, side by side, and an honest line about what we cannot vouch for.

04 — OUTPUT B

The city

Exposure multiplies the repair priority. Forty streets ranked by how many people walk them after dark, exported as a CSV a crew can be dispatched against.

The duality is the load-bearing idea and worth saying out loud in the pitch: exposure multiplies for repair and divides for routing. Same number, opposite sign, because a busy street is where you want to walk and also where a broken lamp costs the most.

Walk home

Pedestrian

The routing half of what the problem statement asks for. Two routes drawn together, two cards, one decision. Never blocks, never alarms — the shortest way is always shown next to the better-lit way, and the person chooses.

The honesty line is deliberate and load-bearing: “the better-lit way is about 3 minutes longer and keeps you off 846 m of street we have no data for.” It quantifies the detour in the unit the walker actually feels, and admits ignorance in the same breath.

Shownbetter-lit 1.85 km / 23 min vs shortest 1.58 km / 20 min
Router ruleavoids the least-certain fifth of the network. Uncertainty is a control signal, not a disclaimer.
Hard findingon the 25 worst segments, 6 have no walkable alternative at all and 19 need a detour over 3× (median 17.9×). Routing cannot fix the worst streets — only repair can.

Report

Sensor

The reporting half. One tap, no account, no lamp number to hunt for in the dark — because nobody standing on an unlit road at 23:00 can identify a lamp post, and asking them to sign up first guarantees no data.

Two things make it more than a complaint box. It suggests where to walk — streets that are busy after dark and where we are only guessing, so a report there actually changes a repair decision. And it closes the loop with the one thing civic apps never give you: “your report moved Outer Ring Road from #24 to #18 in Agaram’s repair queue.”

Targetingupper-confidence-bound, not raw variance. Measured to beat random from 10 reports up.
Verification“we were told this was fixed — is it?” Closure and verification are separate states, so a ward can be told what share of its 96.4% nobody ever confirmed.
Why UCBour first version used exposure × variance and lost to random. It asked about streets no crew would ever visit.

City queue

Municipality

The buyer’s screen, and the only one where density is correct — a desk, a Monday morning, forty rows to dispatch against. Toggle our order against complaint order and the headline ratio recomputes live.

The output is a CSV work order, not a view. A dashboard that can only be looked at is the documented failure mode of this entire category, so the screen ends in a downloadable artifact a crew can be sent against.

Where it is weak

Honest read

You are right that it reads half-finished, and the reason is not polish — it is that three specific decisions are wrong. Ranked by how much they cost us in a demo, with the fix cost beside each.

The map does not look like Bengaluru

20 lines

This is the worst one, and worse than you said. We render bare GeoJSON linework with no street labels, no place names, no water, no parks — because I chose offline-with-no-tile-server so hackathon wifi could never kill the demo. The cost of that choice is that a judge cannot recognise their own city, so the map reads as abstract scribble and quietly undermines every number next to it.

Fix: the data is already there. 1,550 of 2,117 scored segments carry a name property. A MapLibre symbol layer with text-field: ['get','name'] and symbol-placement: 'line' labels the whole network with zero new data and zero network calls. Ward labels come from the same source.

Nothing on any screen says this generalises

one screen

Also right. The generality argument exists only in docs/positioning.md and assets/diagram-inputs.png — none of it is in the prototype. Every visible label in the app is a Bengaluru ward, and the header literally reads “Municipal Engineer Console” with no city named as a parameter. A judge sees a Bengaluru app and asks the obvious question.

Fix: the honest version, not a fake city switcher. Put the input ladder in the app: a coverage panel that shows a road network is the only hard requirement, with each optional source narrowing the bands. Then say the true sentence — we ran it here because Bengaluru publishes unusually good open data; portability is an argument from architecture, and we have not run a second city. That reads as rigour. A greyed-out city dropdown reads as a lie.

The Live feed asks you to read three moving numbers at once

recut copy

This is why the simulation feels half-baked. It is correct and illegible: at any moment the panel has a percentage, a street count, a swap count and roughly ninety words of prose stacked in a 350-pixel column, all changing together. Nobody watching a demo can hold that. The insight — that the worklist barely moves — is genuinely good and currently buried in paragraph four.

Fix: one number at a time. Lead with the walker percentage as a single large figure that visibly falls, move the city count onto the map as a labelled counter, and cut the prose to one sentence that only appears once the run finishes.

City queue front-loads six controls

a disclosure

Budget slider, three weight sliders, an order toggle and a reports slider — all before the screen has told you anything. Density is correct for this surface, but not at first contact.

Fix: lead with the ratio and the top five rows. Put the three weight sliders behind a “tune weights” disclosure. They are a brilliant answer to a judge’s question and a terrible first impression.

Three validation legs are still unmeasured

the real work

Not a bug — flagged as unmeasured everywhere, which is the right call. But it is the challenge question, so it stays open: criterion validation against the two BTP crash PDFs already sitting in data/raw/, field pedestrian counts, and transfer to a second city. The first is the highest-value few hours left in the project — it needs a highlighter, not a parser.

What is actually solid

keep

Walk home and Report — the two screens the problem statement actually names — are both clean, and neither has a jargon problem. One button, two cards. Do not touch them.

The counterfactual survived four independent attacks, including deleting every lamp label we hold, so the win is not a coverage artefact. The bypass finding (routing cannot save the worst streets) is a better answer than the one we set out to give. Mobile works at 414 px. And the whole thing runs offline from precomputed files with one runtime dependency.
