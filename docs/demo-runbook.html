00

Open with the problem

20 sec · no screen

Do not open with “we built a dark-zone map”. The problem is the interesting part, and it frames everything after it as a fix rather than a feature.

Say

BBMP closes 96.4% of electrical complaints. They are good at fixing. The queue is the problem: it is sorted by who complained first, and complaining tracks app-literacy and car ownership — not how many people walk that street at 11pm.

A first-come queue is a competent machine pointed the wrong way. Nobody has to work harder or buy anything. They need the list in a different order.

01

This is Bengaluru after dark

45 sec

Already on Map. Say nothing for three seconds and let them look.

Say

Every ward boundary in Bengaluru — all 225. The dashed rectangle is what we measured: 24 wards, about 0.77 million people. The rest is context.

Colour is how likely a street is to be unlit. Width is how many people walk it after dark. So the bright thick line across the middle is the Outer Ring Road service corridor — dark and heavily walked. That is the whole product in one glance.

Click one street on the amber corridor.

The point to land: this is not a map of broken lamps. It is broken lamps weighted by who is exposed to them. That distinction is the differentiator the problem statement asks for — a lamp-outage map already exists as open data.

02

Here is why the queue is wrong

75 sec · the beat that wins it

Click “See how reports change this”. Let them read the two columns.

Say

Same city, same night, two worklists. On the left, first-come ordering. All five repairs land in one ward — BTM Layout, because BTM Layout files the most complaints. On the right, ours: the ORR service roads in Bellanduru and Marathahalli, where people are actually walking.

Click “Simulate reports”. Let it run.

Say

Reports arriving the way they really arrive — commercial, app-literate streets first. That bias is not a flaw in the demo. It is the thing we exist to correct.

Let it finish. Then read the reveal.

Say

The walker's unknown ground fell from 63% to 6% — citizen reports genuinely fix what a pedestrian can see. But the top 40 repairs changed by one street, because 1,200 reports came from places we already knew about.

That is the finding. Reports alone do not fix the ordering. Crowdsourcing more complaints just amplifies the existing bias. Fixing the order needs the exposure model.

Do not rush this, and do not apologise for the worklist barely moving. It looks like a null result and it is the argument. Then close the overlay.

03

What a person gets

40 sec

Click Walk.

Say

Two ways home. Better lit is 1.85 km, shortest is 1.58. Three minutes longer, and it keeps you off 846 metres of street we have no lighting data for.

Note what this does not do. It does not hide the short route, and it does not throw a safety warning. It offers an alternative and states honestly what we do not know. We avoid what we cannot see; we never certify that a street is safe.

If asked how routing works: same graph, two Dijkstra runs — one weighted by metres, one by a penalty built from darkness, exposure and uncertainty. Wide-band segments are priced at 9× rather than deleted, so a route always exists. It detours; it never fails.

04

And this is how the data arrives

35 sec

Click Report, then the big button.

Say

One tap. No account, no lamp number — nobody standing on an unlit road at 23:00 can identify a lamp post, and asking them to sign up first guarantees no data.

Then the thing no civic app does: your report moved this street from #70 to #44 in its ward's repair queue. Not “thank you, ticket logged”.

And then — the city says this was fixed, is it? Closure and repair are different things, and this is the only place anybody gets asked which one actually happened. It means a ward can be told what share of that 96.4% nobody ever confirmed on the street.

06

Generality, with its limit attached

20 sec · or on demand

Click Coverage.

Say

A road network is the only hard requirement. Everything else narrows the confidence bands — none of it unlocks a feature. We ran it on Bengaluru because Bengaluru publishes unusually good open data.

We have not run a second city. Portability is an argument from architecture, not a demonstration. We would rather say that than show you a dropdown that does nothing.

Volunteering the limit before being asked is worth more than the claim.

If someone asks about phones — hand them yours. 414 px, all four tabs reachable, no clipping. The citizen half of this product is used one-handed on a dark street, which is why the whole thing is dark-first: the alternative is a white rectangle in someone's face on an unlit road.

07

The questions you will get

prepare all five

“How would you validate a footfall estimate where no official data exists?”

The problem statement's own challenge question — prepare this hardest.

Three steps. Estimate from observable proxies: road class, connectivity, night-active land use, transit access. Validate by counting a sample — 10 to 20 streets spanning the predicted range, by hand or camera, prediction against observation. Calibrate, and report the residual rather than burying it.

But the important part: we do not need a correct footfall number. We need an ordering good enough to beat complaint order. So the number to look at is not our headline — it is what survives taking our evidence away. Delete every lamp label we hold and it is still 5.42×. Hold geography and coverage constant and it is 6.4×.

Do not answer this with “beta-binomial posterior”. That answers a question nobody asked.

“Isn't 7.7× just an easy baseline?”

Four stress tests. Against the mean baseline, 7.73×. Against the luckiest of 300 draws, 4.88×. Against a steelman that picks the worst street in every ward it visits, 1.89×. With all lamp labels deleted, 5.42×. It is not a coverage artefact.

“94% of your segments have no lamp evidence — so you're guessing?”

Yes, and the product says so on every screen. That is why every street carries a confidence band, and why the band changes behaviour: the router avoids the least-certain fifth of the network, and the report screen deliberately sends people to it. Uncertainty is a control signal, not a disclaimer.

“Why routing at all — why not just fix the worst streets?”

We tested that. On the 25 worst segments, 6 have no walkable alternative at all, and 19 need a detour over 3× — median 17.9×, worst 206.9×. Routing cannot save the worst streets. Only repair can. Which is why the repair queue is the product, and the route is the reason anyone opens the app.

“The screen says 7.8× but your slide says 7.7×.”

The browser recomputes it live with a different RNG than the pipeline. Same method, same 300 draws, about 1% sampling noise. The canonical figure is 7.73×.

08

Traps

read before you walk in

Each of these is a claim that died on measurement, or a habit that undercuts the pitch. They are listed because they are all tempting.

NEVER
Say “2020–2025” about the complaint data. Those 126,974 rows are 1 Jan – 19 Jun 2025.
NEVER
Quote a per-street footfall count. We show a relative index on purpose — an absolute number would invent the exact quantity we just said must be validated.
NEVER
Claim convergent validation. Spearman ρ came out at 0.03 between the two exposure halves. It failed, and the failure is the more honest story.
NEVER
Use the office-hours timestamp argument. The grievance clock is a 12-hour field with AM/PM stripped. The claim is dead.
NEVER
Claim a second city. Under any phrasing.
NEVER
Open the sliders early. They are the answer to a question, not a first impression.
NEVER
Narrate a blank map. Reload before speaking.
